Part 01

Why Shoot RAW?

Every professional photojournalist shoots in RAW format. This is not optional at the top level — it is standard practice at AP, Reuters, Getty Images, the New York Times, and every major publication in the world. Understanding why will make you a better photographer immediately — even before you touch Lightroom.

"Shooting RAW is like keeping the negative. JPEG is like getting someone else's print made from your negative."

— Common wisdom in professional photography

RAW Format (.CR3, .NEF, .ARW)

  • ✓ Contains all data the sensor captured — nothing discarded
  • ✓ White balance fully adjustable after the fact with zero quality loss
  • ✓ Blown highlights and crushed shadows can be recovered
  • ✓ 12–14 bit colour depth (thousands more tones than JPEG)
  • ✓ Non-destructive editing — original file is never altered
  • ✓ Industry standard for professional delivery
  • ✗ Large file size (20–50MB per image)
  • ✗ Cannot be published directly — must be processed first
  • ✗ Requires Lightroom, Camera Raw, or similar software to open

JPEG Format

  • ✓ Small file size — easy to share and store
  • ✓ Ready to publish immediately out of camera
  • ✓ Opens on every device without special software
  • ✗ Camera throws away ~80% of captured data on save
  • ✗ White balance baked in — very difficult to fix after the fact
  • ✗ Blown highlights are permanently lost — no recovery possible
  • ✗ 8-bit colour — visible banding in skies and gradients
  • ✗ Each re-save degrades the file further (lossy compression)
  • ✗ Limited latitude for correction in post-production

How to enable RAW on your camera: On most DSLRs and mirrorless cameras, go to Menu → Image Quality → RAW (or RAW + JPEG if you want both). On Canon cameras it's typically Menu → Image Quality. On Nikon, Menu → Shooting Menu → Image Quality. The exact path varies — check your camera manual or search "[your camera model] how to shoot RAW."

If you're using a smartphone, iPhone and many Android phones can shoot RAW via the native camera app (toggle on in Settings → Camera → Formats → Apple ProRAW on iPhone 12 Pro+) or through apps like Halide or Adobe Lightroom Mobile.

Part 02

Reading the Histogram

The histogram is the most important tool in Lightroom. It tells you the truth about your image's exposure — far more accurately than your screen, which can be misleading depending on brightness settings and ambient light. A professional photographer reads the histogram before they trust their eyes.

The histogram is a graph: the left edge is pure black (shadows), the right edge is pure white (highlights). The peaks show how much of your image lives at each brightness level. Your goal is a histogram that sits comfortably in the middle without slamming into either wall.

Underexposed

Most data piled up on the left. The image is too dark. Shadow detail is lost or "crushed." In RAW, you can often recover this — but not always. Better to expose correctly in camera.

Overexposed

Data slammed against the right wall. Highlights are "blown out" — permanently pure white. Even in RAW, if the sensor never captured the data, you can't recover it. A common mistake with bright skies.

Well Exposed

The bulk of the data sits in the middle of the graph, with gradual tails on both ends. No data is slammed against either wall. This image has maximum editing latitude in Lightroom.

Pro tip: Enable "highlight clipping warnings" in Lightroom by pressing J. Blown-out areas will flash red. This makes it impossible to miss a problem before you export.
Part 03

Step-by-Step Lightroom Workflow

Open your RAW file in Lightroom Classic → go to the Develop module (press D). Work through the panel on the right in the order below. The order matters — changes at the top affect everything below.

1

Develop Module → Basic Panel

Import & Set White Balance

Before anything else, set your white balance. In RAW this is completely lossless — you're not degrading the image at all. Click the White Balance eyedropper (W key) and click on something that should be neutral white or grey in your scene — a white wall, a piece of paper, a grey card. Then fine-tune with the Temp (warm/cool) and Tint (green/magenta) sliders.

White Balance Selector (W)TemperatureTint
Quick start: The dropdown above the sliders offers presets (Daylight, Cloudy, Tungsten, Flash). Try the one that matches your shooting conditions — it's usually faster than the eyedropper for an approximate correction.
2

Basic Panel → Tone

Correct the Exposure

Now address the overall brightness. Watch the histogram as you adjust. The Exposure slider moves everything up or down. Then use the individual tone controls to fine-tune each part of the tonal range independently.

ExposureHighlightsShadowsWhitesBlacks

The standard order: drag Highlights left (toward -100) to recover a blown sky. Drag Shadows right (toward +100) to open up dark areas without affecting bright areas. Set Whites and Blacks last — hold Alt/Option while dragging to see exactly where clipping starts.

The "S-Curve" of tonal adjustment: A common starting point — Highlights: -40, Shadows: +30, Whites: +15, Blacks: -10 — creates a gentle contrast lift that looks natural in most photojournalism situations.
3

Basic Panel → Presence

Add Contrast, Texture & Clarity

The Contrast slider deepens the difference between light and dark globally. Used subtly (±15 to ±25), it adds punch. The Clarity slider increases local mid-tone contrast — excellent for bringing out texture in skin, fabric, rock, bark, and brick. Texture is a finer-grained version of Clarity — great for subtle detail enhancement without the "crunchy" look Clarity can produce if overdone.

ContrastClarityTextureVibrance
Photojournalism rule: If it visibly looks processed, you've gone too far. Keep Clarity under +30. Negative Clarity (-20 to -40) can beautifully smooth skin in portraiture while retaining a natural look. Avoid Vibrance boosts above +25 — they make images look artificially saturated.
4

Develop Panel → Crop Overlay

Crop & Straighten

Press R to open the Crop Overlay tool. Drag the corners to crop. Use the angle slider or drag outside the crop box to straighten a tilted horizon. The Auto button attempts to auto-straighten — it works well for clear horizons and architecture.

R — Crop ToolConstrain to ImageAuto Straighten
Rule of thirds overlay: While the crop tool is active, press O to cycle through overlay guides including the rule of thirds grid. Use it to check your placement before confirming the crop.
Remember: Cropping is acceptable in photojournalism. Removing objects is not. The Crop tool is fine. The Healing Brush, Clone Stamp, and Content-Aware Fill are not for editorial work.
5

Optional — Black & White

Convert to Monochrome

If your brief calls for it, converting to black and white can add weight and timelessness to a photograph. In Lightroom, go to Basic Panel → Treatment and select Black & White — or press V. This gives you access to the B&W Mix panel, where you can independently brighten or darken specific colour ranges as they convert to grey. This is far more powerful than just desaturating in Photoshop.

V — B&W ToggleB&W Mix PanelTargeted Adjustment Tool

Common B&W adjustments: drag the Blue slider left to darken skies dramatically (classic photojournalism look). Drag Red right to brighten skin tones. Use the Targeted Adjustment Tool (click in the image and drag) to adjust whatever colour you're clicking on.

6

Optional — Noise Reduction

Clean Up High-ISO Images

If you shot in low light with a high ISO (1600, 3200, 6400+), your image may show digital noise — a grainy, speckled texture especially visible in dark areas. In Lightroom, go to Detail Panel → Noise Reduction → Luminance. Drag it right until the noise becomes acceptable, then dial back until you're not losing too much fine detail. Start around 25–40.

Detail PanelLuminance NRDetailContrast
Lightroom AI Denoise (2023+): If you have a recent version of Lightroom Classic, try Denoise under the Detail panel. It uses AI to remove noise while preserving significantly more detail than the manual sliders. Click it, choose a strength, and wait ~30 seconds. It's remarkable on high-ISO images.
Part 04

The Sliders — Quick Reference

The Basic panel sliders each control a specific part of the tonal range. This quick reference shows their typical range and what they do in plain language.

Exposure
-5 EV+5 EV

Overall image brightness. One stop = one full exposure change. Start here.

Highlights
-100+100

Controls only the brightest areas. Drag left to recover blown-out skies. Doesn't affect shadows.

Shadows
-100+100

Controls only the darkest areas. Drag right to open up dark areas without affecting bright areas.

Whites
-100+100

Sets the white point. Hold Alt/Option while dragging to see exactly where clipping starts.

Blacks
-100+100

Sets the black point. Drag left to make blacks deeper and add contrast. Alt/Option shows clipping.

Contrast
-100+100

Deepens the gap between light and dark globally. Keep it subtle in journalism (±20).

Clarity
-100+100

Mid-tone contrast. Adds punch to textures. Keep under +30 to avoid an "over-processed" look.

Vibrance
-100+100

Boosts muted colours more than already-saturated ones. Gentler than Saturation. Keep under +25.

Part 05

Practice Exercise

The best way to learn Lightroom is to fix a problem. Below are three scenarios — find or create an image that matches each one (or your teacher may provide sample images), then correct it using only the techniques covered in this tutorial.

Three Correction Challenges

Complete at least two of the three. Record your adjustments in your editing log.

Challenge A

The Dark Interior

Find or shoot an interior photograph that is noticeably underexposed — taken in a cafeteria, classroom, or gym with mixed or low lighting. Your goal is to bring it to a naturally exposed-looking result.

  • Correct white balance (interior light is usually warm/tungsten)
  • Lift Exposure and open Shadows without blowing highlights
  • Apply Noise Reduction if high ISO was needed
  • Record every adjustment with its value in your editing log

Challenge B

The Blown Sky

Find or shoot an outdoor photograph where the sky is completely blown out — pure white with no cloud or sky detail. This is one of the most common problems in photography.

  • Drag Highlights to -80 or lower and see how much detail returns
  • Compensate by lifting Shadows and Exposure slightly
  • Discuss in your log: was the sky recoverable, or was the data simply never captured?
  • Note the histogram before and after

Challenge C

The Wrong Light

Find or shoot an image with clearly wrong white balance — taken under fluorescent school lighting (green-tinted), tungsten bulbs (very orange), or with the wrong preset selected in-camera.

  • Use the White Balance eyedropper on a neutral area
  • Fine-tune with Temperature and Tint sliders
  • Compare the corrected version to the original
  • Convert to B&W and note how the tones change vs. the colour version

What to Submit

  • The original RAW or JPEG file (before)
  • The exported corrected JPEG (after)
  • Your editing log entry for each challenge — adjustment name, value, and reason
  • One sentence: what did this challenge teach you that you'll apply in your photojournalism project?
Part 06

Exporting Your Final Images

When your edits are done, you export a finished JPEG from Lightroom. The original RAW file is never touched. This is the non-destructive workflow — your "negative" is always preserved. Use the settings below for your photojournalism project submission.

Export Settings — Submission

  • File Format: JPEG
  • Quality: 90–100
  • Color Space: sRGB
  • Image Sizing: Long Edge 3000px minimum (or Don't Enlarge, full resolution)
  • Resolution: 300 PPI
  • Metadata: Copyright Only
  • Filename: Brief01_LastName.jpg

File Naming Convention

  • Brief01_Smith.jpg
  • Brief02_Smith.jpg
  • Brief03_Smith.jpg
  • Keep all RAW originals in a folder named RAW_Originals_LastName
  • Submit the RAW folder alongside your JPEG exports
  • Your RAW files are proof that no unethical manipulation occurred
Lightroom Catalogue backup: Go to Edit → Catalog Settings → Backup and set it to "Once a day, when exiting Lightroom." One corrupted catalogue losing your edit history is a lesson no one needs to learn the hard way.
Part 07

The Ethics Reminder

You now have access to powerful tools. This section is a reminder of where the professional line is — because Lightroom also contains tools that go beyond it.

Use These Freely

  • ✓ Exposure, Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks
  • ✓ White Balance (Temperature, Tint)
  • ✓ Contrast, Clarity, Texture, Vibrance
  • ✓ Crop and Straighten
  • ✓ Black & White conversion
  • ✓ Noise Reduction
  • ✓ Lens Corrections (Remove Chromatic Aberration, Enable Profile Corrections)

Do Not Use for Editorial Work

  • ✗ Healing Brush / Clone Stamp (removes objects)
  • ✗ Content-Aware Remove
  • ✗ Selective Hue/Saturation changes (changing colour of specific objects)
  • ✗ AI-generated sky replacements
  • ✗ Compositing (combining multiple images)
  • ✗ Extreme localized adjustments that change what an area looks like
  • ✗ Photoshop Generative Fill on any area of the image
