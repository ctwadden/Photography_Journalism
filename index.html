Section 01

What is Photojournalism?

A photojournalist is a storyteller with a camera. Unlike portrait or commercial photographers, the photojournalist's job is singular: to capture truth in a single frame.

That image might document a moment of joy, grief, injustice, or quiet humanity. It might be published in a newspaper tomorrow, or end up in a museum fifty years from now. What all photojournalism shares is a commitment to the real — to something that actually happened, in a moment that actually existed.

Photojournalists work on assignment. A photo editor identifies a story that needs images and hands the photographer a brief — a short description of the story, the feeling to capture, and a deadline. For this project, your “field” is the school building. You will find the story in real school life rather than leaving campus.

Professional photojournalists work for wire services like Associated Press (AP), Reuters, and Getty Images — whose photographs appear in virtually every newspaper on the planet. The Nova Scotia tradition runs through the Chronicle Herald, where photographers have documented this province for generations.

"Photography is the simplest thing in the world, but it is incredibly complicated to make it really work."

— Martin Parr, Documentary Photographer

What a Photo Desk Looks Like

  • Photo Editor — assigns photographers, selects published images, manages the visual language of the publication
  • Staff Photographer — covers assigned stories on deadline
  • Freelance Photographer — independent contractor hired per story
  • Photo Researcher — finds archival images, manages rights clearances

A Day in the Field

  • Receive a brief from the photo editor
  • Research the story — who, what, where, when
  • Scout the location if possible
  • Arrive early; spend time with subjects before shooting
  • Shoot in RAW; take hundreds of frames
  • Return; edit down to 5–10 selects
  • Process within ethical limits; deliver with captions
Section 02

Photographers Worth Knowing

Before you go out and make photographs, study the people who defined the craft. Each link below leads to a real image, archive, or documentary — primary sources, not summaries. Spend a few minutes here before you pick your briefs.

HCB

Henri Cartier-Bresson

1908–2004 · France

The father of modern photojournalism. He coined the "decisive moment" — the precise instant when all visual and emotional elements align. He shot almost exclusively with a small Leica, often unnoticed, and believed in never cropping after the fact. His work defined what a serious photograph looks like for the next 75 years.

Magnum Photos Archive Documentary: "The Decisive Moment" (YouTube) ICP Archive
Decisive Moment · Humanist Photography · Magnum Founder
DL

Dorothea Lange

1895–1965 · USA

Migrant Mother (1936) became the defining image of the Great Depression — a single portrait that communicated an entire social catastrophe. Lange worked for the US Farm Security Administration, documenting poverty across America. She built trust with her subjects before she ever raised her camera. Her work proved that one photograph could change public policy.

MoMA Collection Migrant Mother — Library of Congress Documentary short (YouTube)
Documentary · Social Reform · FSA
NU

Nick Ut

b. 1951 · Vietnam/USA

AP photographer who shot The Terror of War in 1972 — a photograph of nine-year-old Kim Phúc running from a napalm attack. The image helped turn American opinion against the Vietnam War. Ut then put down his camera, rushed Phúc to hospital, and saved her life. He worked for AP for over 50 years. His story is a lesson in the weight of a single frame.

Story Behind the Photo — Magnum Nick Ut & Kim Phúc — reunion documentary AP News — retirement retrospective
War Photography · AP · Pulitzer Prize 1973
LA

Lynsey Addario

b. 1973 · USA

One of the most important conflict photographers working today. Addario has covered wars in Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, and Sudan, and the global refugee crisis. She was kidnapped in Libya in 2011 and returned to the field days after her release. Her memoir It's What I Do is essential reading. She shoots for the New York Times and National Geographic.

Official Portfolio — lynseyaddario.com TED Talk: Photojournalism in Conflict Zones National Geographic profile
Conflict & Humanitarian · NY Times · MacArthur Fellow
WE

William Eggleston

b. 1939 · USA

Eggleston legitimized colour photography as serious art. Before his 1976 MoMA show, colour was considered commercial. He photographed gas stations, parking lots, and suburban lawns — and found the extraordinary in the completely ordinary. His lesson for you: the mundane world around you right now is full of worthy subjects. You don't need a war zone.

MoMA — William Eggleston Collection Eggleston Artistic Trust Documentary: "The Colourful Mr. Eggleston"
Colour Photography · The Everyday · Democratic Forest
YK

Yunghi Kim

b. 1960 · Korea/USA

A pioneering photojournalist who covered humanitarian crises including Rwanda and the collapse of Somalia. Kim was among the first photographers on the ground in Rwanda in 1994. She is also a vocal advocate for women in photojournalism and has turned her camera on her own identity and cultural experience — proof that personal stories can carry the same weight as international ones.

Section 03

Your Assignment

You are working like a photographer for a school newspaper, yearbook, or student media team. Your job is to find small, positive, real stories happening around the school and make one strong image for each story.

Choose three school article briefs from the eight provided. For each brief, submit one edited photograph, a written caption, and one low-key article add-on. You do not need to leave school property for this assignment.

What You Submit

  • Three school article briefs chosen from the eight provided
  • One hero photograph per brief — your single best image for that school story
  • One polished caption per photograph using the caption guide below
  • One low-key article add-on per brief: a short quote, two quick facts, or a two-sentence context note
  • Each photo edited in Lightroom using ethical adjustments only: crop, exposure, contrast, white balance, highlights, shadows, sharpening, and colour correction
  • Image files named: Brief01_LastName.jpg, Brief02_LastName.jpg, Brief03_LastName.jpg — minimum 3000px long edge when possible

Keep it simple: You are not writing a full article. You are creating a strong school photo with a caption and one small piece of supporting information. The photo should do most of the storytelling.

Section 05

Composition Fundamentals

A technically perfect exposure means nothing if the composition is weak. Professional photojournalists internalize these principles until they become instinct. You're building those instincts now.

Rule of Thirds

Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid. Place your key subject — or the horizon — along one of the lines or at one of the four intersection points. Off-centre compositions create movement and tension.

Leading Lines

Roads, fences, hallways, rivers — any strong line that draws the eye into the frame creates depth. The best leading lines guide your eye directly to the subject. Look for them everywhere.

Framing Within the Frame

Use doorways, windows, arches, or out-of-focus foreground to create a frame inside your frame. This adds depth, focuses attention, and gives the viewer a sense of discovery.

Depth of Field

Shallow (f/1.4–f/2.8): subject sharp, background blurred. Isolates. Perfect for portraits and intimate moments.

Deep (f/8–f/16): everything sharp front to back. Situates your subject in their world. Used heavily in documentary.

A blurred background says "this person matters above all else." A sharp background says "this person exists in this world."

Light Direction

  • Front light — even, flattering, but flat; lacks texture
  • Side light — reveals texture, adds drama and dimension
  • Back light — silhouettes and rim-lit edges; cinematic
  • Overcast — the best natural softbox; shadow-free, flattering
Section 06

The Ethics of the Frame

Photojournalism is one of the few professions where the tools of the craft can be used to tell truth or to deceive — and where the line between the two isn't always obvious. The industry has strict ethical standards because the stakes of getting it wrong are real. A manipulated image in a news context can destroy reputations, incite violence, or undermine public trust in journalism itself.

These rules are based on the National Press Photographers Association (NPPA) Code of Ethics — the standard framework used globally.

What's Acceptable — What's Not

OK

Adjusting overall exposure, brightness, and contrast — provided adjustments apply to the whole image equally, and the result accurately reflects what was there.

OK

Correcting white balance — making a scene look as it actually appeared to the naked eye, rather than the colour shift introduced by artificial lighting.

OK

Cropping the frame — removing edges to improve composition or remove irrelevant material at the border. Normal part of the editorial process.

OK

Converting to black and white — a tonal, not factual, change. Acceptable provided it is disclosed and does not misrepresent the scene.

NOT OK

Adding or removing objects, people, or elements — includes removing a distracting background object with Healing Brush, cloning out a person, or compositing two images. This is fabrication.

NOT OK

Changing the colour of clothing, objects, or skin — selectively altering hue or saturation to change what something looked like is misrepresentation.

NOT OK

AI-generated or AI-enhanced images submitted as documentary photography — if any element was generated by AI, it is illustration, not journalism.

NOT OK

Staging a scene and presenting it as spontaneous — asking a subject to "act like they're working" and presenting the result as a candid documentary image is fabrication of the event.

These rules have consequences. In 2003, LA Times photographer Brian Walski was fired after compositing two images from the Iraq War to create a stronger composition. Careers that took decades to build ended in a day. Your editing log in the submission template is your ethical accountability document.

Section 07

Writing the Caption

A caption should help the viewer understand the photo without turning the assignment into a full essay. For this project, keep the writing simple, clear, and specific. Your photo does most of the storytelling; your caption gives the reader the missing context.

The Simple Caption Formula

Sentence 1
What is happening? Describe the real moment in present tense. Avoid “This is a picture of...” and avoid stating only the obvious.
Sentence 2
Why does it matter? Connect the image to the school article brief: graduation, future plans, cafeteria prices, making a team, or friendship.
Sentence 3
Photographer choice. Briefly explain one choice you made in the photo or Lightroom edit: crop, angle, light, depth of field, colour, contrast, or black and white.
Add-On
One extra detail. Add one short quote, two quick facts, or a two-sentence context note. Keep it low key.

Caption — Weak

"This is a picture of the cafeteria price board. It shows food prices."

This only tells the viewer what they can already see. It does not connect to the story or explain a photography choice.

Caption — Strong

"Students line up below the cafeteria price board during the lunch rush. The menu shows how everyday food choices are also budget choices for students trying to get through a long school day. I corrected the white balance in Lightroom and cropped tighter so the prices and hands at the counter became the focus."
